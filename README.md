1. Introduction

Every planet in our Solar System has a different distance from the Sun, surface temperature, internal energy, gravitational strength, magnetic environment, and atmospheric composition.

Some planets have carbon dioxide-dominated atmospheres, while others contain predominantly hydrogen and helium. Earth has an atmosphere primarily composed of nitrogen and oxygen.

These differences raise an important scientific question:

How do solar energy, planetary internal heat, and space-environment conditions collectively influence the formation, transformation, and retention of atmospheric gases on planets?

The objective of this study is to examine these natural processes through a unified comparative framework.

2. Central Concept

This study considers three major components:

1. The Sun.
2. The planets.
3. The space environment.

These components are connected through natural processes involving energy, matter, radiation, and gravitational interactions.

The central conceptual idea is that planetary atmospheric composition cannot be explained by solar distance alone. It may result from the combined influence of several physical, chemical, geological, and biological processes.

3. Solar Distance and Energy Reception

The distance of a planet from the Sun determines the amount of solar radiation it receives per unit area, assuming comparable orientation and exposure.

The inverse-square relationship is expressed as:

[
I=\frac{L}{4\pi r^2}
]

Where:

- I = Solar radiation flux.
- L = Total solar luminosity.
- r = Distance from the Sun.

According to this relationship, solar radiation decreases as the square of the distance increases.

Changes in solar energy reception can influence planetary temperatures, surface conditions, water availability, evaporation, and chemical processes.

However, actual planetary temperatures also depend on atmospheric composition, reflectivity, greenhouse effects, and internal heat.

4. Planetary Internal Heat

Planets are not dependent exclusively on solar energy.

They may also contain internal energy originating from several natural sources:

- Radioactive decay.
- Primordial heat remaining from planetary formation.
- Gravitational compression and internal differentiation.
- Tidal heating in certain planetary systems.

Internal heat can influence volcanic activity, geological transformations, and the release of gases from planetary interiors.

Volcanic processes, for example, can release water vapour, carbon dioxide, and sulfur dioxide.

Therefore, internal energy must be considered separately when studying planetary atmospheric composition.

5. The Role of the Space Environment

An important component of this research is the role of space itself.

Space is not completely empty. It contains several natural components and physical processes, including:

- Solar wind.
- Electrically charged particles.
- Electric and magnetic fields.
- Solar and cosmic radiation.
- Extremely low particle density and pressure.
- Gravitational interactions between celestial bodies.

These conditions can interact with planetary atmospheres.

In particular, solar wind and energetic particles can influence the upper atmospheric regions of planets.

Under certain conditions, atmospheric particles can escape into space. This process is known as atmospheric escape.

Therefore, understanding planetary atmospheres requires examining not only how gases originate but also how they are retained, transformed, and lost.

6. Gravity and Atmospheric Retention

A planet's mass and gravitational strength influence its ability to retain atmospheric gases.

The thermal motion of gas molecules depends partly on temperature and molecular mass.

Under suitable conditions, some atmospheric particles can reach escape velocities or enter other escape processes and leave the planet.

Important factors include:

- Planetary mass.
- Planetary radius.
- Gravitational escape velocity.
- Atmospheric temperature.
- Molecular mass of gases.
- Solar radiation and solar wind.

An important research question is:

To what extent does planetary gravitational strength explain differences in atmospheric gas retention among planets?

7. Magnetic Fields and Solar Wind

Planetary magnetic fields interact with the solar wind.

Earth's global magnetic field forms a magnetosphere that interacts with charged particles arriving from the Sun.

Mars does not currently possess an Earth-like global intrinsic magnetic field, although certain ancient surface rocks preserve evidence of localized remanent magnetism.

Venus also lacks an Earth-like intrinsic global magnetic field.

These differences provide opportunities to compare planetary atmospheric evolution.

The purpose of this investigation is to examine how magnetic environments and solar-wind interactions influence atmospheric processes and atmospheric escape.

8. Formation and Transformation of Atmospheric Gases

Atmospheric gases can originate through several natural processes.

Major processes include:

1. Volcanic outgassing.
2. Thermal decomposition of materials.
3. Chemical reactions.
4. Evaporation of water and other volatile substances.
5. Photochemical reactions.
6. Biological activities.
7. Retention of gases acquired during planetary formation.

Examples:

- Carbon dioxide can originate from geological and chemical processes.
- Photosynthesis is the major biological source of atmospheric oxygen on Earth.
- Methane can originate through both biological and abiotic processes.
- Hydrogen and helium are associated with planetary formation and atmospheric retention.

Consequently, the presence of a particular gas cannot be explained solely by planetary temperature or solar distance.

Venus, Earth, and Mars are selected as the initial subjects for comparative investigation.

Comparative Observations

Venus

Venus is closer to the Sun than Earth and has an extremely hot surface.

Its atmosphere is dense and predominantly composed of carbon dioxide.

Its powerful greenhouse effect contributes to its exceptionally high surface temperature.

Earth

Earth receives a moderate amount of solar energy compared with Venus and Mars.

It has liquid-water environments, biological activity, and an atmosphere dominated by nitrogen and oxygen.

Photosynthesis, geological processes, and the carbon cycle have played important roles in the development of Earth's present atmospheric composition.

Mars

Mars is farther from the Sun than Earth.

Its atmosphere is very thin and predominantly composed of carbon dioxide.

Its atmospheric evolution is associated with its relatively low gravity, ancient climate, solar-wind interactions, and atmospheric escape.

Initial Comparative Observation

The three planets demonstrate that solar distance and temperature are important, but they do not independently determine atmospheric composition.

Venus and Mars both have carbon dioxide-dominated atmospheres despite substantial differences in temperature, pressure, and solar distance.

This difference provides an important starting point for further investigation.

10. Proposed Conceptual Mathematical Framework

For comparative analysis, the following relationship is proposed:

[
G=f(E_s,D,H_i,T,P,g,M,S,C)
]

Where:

- G = Atmospheric gas composition and abundance.
- Es = Solar energy.
- D = Distance from the Sun.
- Hi = Planetary internal heat.
- T = Temperature.
- P = Atmospheric pressure.
- g = Gravitational acceleration.
- M = Available source materials.
- S = Space-environment conditions and solar-wind interactions.
- C = Chemical and biological processes.

This is a proposed conceptual framework, not an established mathematical law.

Its purpose is to identify the independent and combined contributions of different factors and to provide a basis for future quantitative modelling.

11. Interactions Between Planets

Planets revolve around the Sun in separate orbital paths.

Their relative positions continuously change.

Every planet exerts a gravitational influence on other planets.

However, the amount of sunlight blocked by one planet before it reaches another is generally negligible.

Therefore, this study separates planetary interactions into two categories:

1. Gravitational and orbital interactions between planets.
2. Solar-energy reception and atmospheric processes.

These two processes should not be treated as directly connected without supporting evidence.

12. Major Research Questions

This study aims to investigate the following questions:

1. What is the relationship between solar distance and the amount of energy received by a planet?
2. How does temperature influence the formation and retention of atmospheric gases?
3. To what extent does planetary internal heat contribute to gas release?
4. How does the low-pressure space environment relate to atmospheric escape?
5. How do solar-wind interactions and magnetic fields influence atmospheric evolution?
6. What is the relationship between planetary gravity and the molecular mass of atmospheric gases?
7. Why do planets at different solar distances contain some of the same gases?
8. To what extent do the materials available during planetary formation determine present atmospheric composition?
9. How do biological activities transform planetary atmospheres?
10. Can these factors be represented through a combined mathematical model?

13. Research Methodology

The study will proceed through the following stages.

Stage 1 — Planetary Distance

Compare the mean solar distances of all eight planets and calculate their relative solar-energy reception.

Stage 2 — Temperature and Internal Heat

Examine planetary temperatures, internal energy sources, and surface conditions.

Stage 3 — Atmospheric Gas Composition

Compare the major and trace gases present in the atmospheres of all eight planets.

Stage 4 — Gravity and Magnetic Fields

Investigate atmospheric retention, solar-wind interactions, and atmospheric escape.

Stage 5 — Space Environment

Examine radiation, solar wind, charged particles, and the space environment surrounding each planet.

Stage 6 — Chemical and Biological Processes

Identify the natural sources and transformation processes responsible for atmospheric gases.

Stage 7 — Mathematical Comparison

Analyse available planetary data to identify individual and combined effects and evaluate the proposed conceptual framework.

14. Research Limitations

This research is currently at the conceptual and hypothesis-development stage.

The proposed ideas should not be considered established scientific conclusions.

Understanding actual planetary atmospheres requires reliable measurements from space missions, telescopes, spectroscopy, planetary science, geology, and physics.

An observed relationship between one factor and atmospheric composition does not automatically establish a causal relationship.

The individual and combined effects of different variables must be investigated through quantitative analysis, physical models, and independent testing.

15. Research Objective

The central objective is to understand planets not as isolated bodies but through their natural interactions with the Sun and the surrounding space environment.

This study seeks to investigate how energy flow, available matter, temperature, gravity, magnetic fields, and chemical processes collectively influence planetary atmospheric composition.

The broader aim is to develop a comparative conceptual framework that can be subjected to independent scientific review, mathematical analysis, and future testing.

16. Concluding Perspective

A planet's natural state cannot be explained by its distance from the Sun alone.

Its orbital position, solar-energy reception, internal structure, available materials, and interactions with the space environment collectively influence its physical and atmospheric conditions.

The formation, transformation, retention, and escape of planetary gases are interconnected natural processes.

Under Action-Flow Theory, this research attempts to examine these relationships and develop a conceptual model that can be independently evaluated through scientific investigation.
